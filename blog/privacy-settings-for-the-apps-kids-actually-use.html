Privacy Settings for the Apps Kids Actually Use: Snapchat, TikTok, Discord, Roblox

Wednesday, May 20, 2026

Earlier in this series we covered the warning signs parents should actually watch for and the network and web-filter layer at home. This post is the device-and-app layer: the privacy settings inside the four apps that show up most on a kid's phone.

The goal here is not to ban apps. It is to set them up the way they should have shipped — private by default, contact limited to people the child actually knows, and the most exploitable features turned off.

TL;DR

Each of these apps has reasonable safety settings, but most of them are buried under several menus and turned off by default. Turn on the family or parental link where it exists, lock the account to private, limit who can contact your child, and review settings together every few months. Specific links to each platform's official help page are below.

Why default settings are not enough

Apps are built to maximize engagement. That is not a moral failure — it is the business model. The settings that protect a kid almost always reduce engagement, which is why they ship off by default and live three or four taps deep.

The practical implication: a parent who never opens the settings menu has effectively chosen the most permissive option for their child. Spending twenty minutes once is the difference between a child who can be DMed by anyone and one who cannot.

Two more reasons to review on purpose: app updates occasionally reset privacy choices, and shared family devices often end up with second accounts that skip the parent-supervised setup entirely.

Snapchat

Snapchat is the platform most associated with private, short-lived messaging — which also makes its safety controls the ones most worth getting right. The biggest single move is enabling Family Center, Snapchat's official parental-supervision tool, which lets a parent see who their teen is messaging without seeing the message contents.

Beyond Family Center, the controls that matter most are: setting Contact Me and View My Story to Friends only; turning off location sharing on the Snap Map (the "Ghost Mode" toggle); and disabling Quick Add so strangers cannot find the account through mutual contacts. Snapchat documents the full process at the official Snapchat Family Center.

TikTok

TikTok's parent-side feature is called Family Pairing. A parent links their own account to the child's and can then set screen-time limits, restrict direct messages, and turn on Restricted Mode (a content filter) from their own phone. Under-16 accounts have DMs disabled by default — keep it that way.

The other settings worth confirming on the child's account: account set to private, downloads disabled on their videos, comments restricted to friends, and the suggest-account-to-others toggle off. TikTok's own walkthrough lives at the TikTok Guardian's Guide.

Discord

Discord is harder than the others because it is server-based — a child's exposure depends on which communities they have joined, not just on their account settings. The platform now offers a Family Center that gives parents a weekly summary of new friends, server joins, and direct-message activity without exposing message content.

On the account itself, set the content filter to its strictest option, set "Allow direct messages from server members" to off, and limit friend requests to "Friends of friends" or "None." For younger users, review the server list together — most of the real risk lives in public servers, not in DMs from strangers. Discord's parent-facing documentation is at Discord Family Center.

Roblox

Roblox is technically a game, but for most kids it functions as a social platform with a built-in store. The two settings that change the most are an accurate account age (under-13 accounts get stricter chat and content rules automatically) and a Parent PIN that locks the settings menu so the child cannot loosen them later.

From there: set the content maturity level appropriately, restrict chat to friends or off entirely for younger kids, turn on monthly spending limits or remove the saved payment method, and enable two-step verification on the account. Roblox keeps its current parent guidance at the Roblox Parents page.

Settings drift is real

The biggest threat to a properly set-up app is time. Updates add new features — sometimes with their own opt-in defaults. Settings occasionally revert. A child upgrades to a new phone and the install starts over. A friend's account on a shared device skips the supervision step.

A short quarterly check fixes most of this. Walk through each app together once a season — not as an inspection, just as part of how the household runs. Use the same calendar reminder you would use for any other recurring chore.

The five settings to verify first on any app

  • Account is set to private (or its closest equivalent).
  • Friend requests and DMs are limited to known contacts, or disabled.
  • Location sharing is off.
  • The platform's official family or parental-supervision feature is enabled where one exists.
  • Two-step verification is turned on so the account itself cannot be hijacked.
