A Small-Business Patching Rhythm That Actually Sticks

Wednesday, May 6, 2026

Most small-business breaches we read about every month start the same way: a known vulnerability, a patch that has been available for weeks, and nobody whose job it was to install it.

Patching is not glamorous, and it is rarely on a calendar. That is exactly why it slips. The good news is you do not need an enterprise tool stack to stay current — you need a small, predictable rhythm and one person who owns it.

TL;DR

Pick one recurring hour each month, patch in a fixed order (servers and network gear, then workstations, then apps), verify with a short checklist, and write down what you skipped and why. That is 80 percent of what enterprise patch management actually delivers.

Why patching falls off

For most small offices and ministries, the pattern is the same. Updates pop up at the worst possible time, someone clicks "remind me later," and a few months go by. When something does break, the fix often turns out to be a patch that was published last quarter.

  • Nobody is named as the owner, so it becomes everyone's job and nobody's job.
  • Updates interrupt work mid-day, so users defer them indefinitely.
  • Network gear and printers get forgotten because they are "just working."
  • There is no record of what was patched, so the next time the question comes up, the answer is a guess.

A simple monthly rhythm

Patch Tuesday — the second Tuesday of the month — is when Microsoft and several other major vendors publish their updates. That gives you a natural anchor. Pick a window that works for your team, then keep it the same every month so it shows up on the calendar without being negotiated.

  • Second Wednesday, after hours: apply server, firewall, and network device updates. These are the highest-impact targets and they need a maintenance window anyway.
  • Second Thursday, end of day: push workstation updates and reboot. A scheduled reboot at 5:30 PM with a friendly notice beats fighting with users at 10 AM.
  • Third Monday morning: review third-party apps — browsers, PDF readers, accounting software, point-of-sale, line-of-business tools. These are often the vector attackers actually use.

What to actually patch

Workstation operating systems are usually the first thing people think of, but they are not where most small-business compromises start. Build your list once and keep it short.

  • Windows and macOS, including drivers for laptops that travel.
  • Microsoft 365 / Office, browsers (Chrome, Edge, Firefox), Adobe Reader, Zoom, Teams.
  • Servers and any virtual machines, including the hypervisor.
  • Firewalls, switches, access points, and the office router. Vendor security advisories for these have been busy in 2026.
  • Backup software and the backup appliance itself — the place attackers go second.
  • Line-of-business apps: accounting, scheduling, donor or member database, point-of-sale.

Verify, do not assume

The most common patching failure we see is silent: the update was downloaded, the reboot never happened, and nobody noticed for six months. A two-minute verification at the end of each cycle prevents most of that.

  • Spot-check three or four machines and confirm the build number changed.
  • Open the firewall admin page and confirm it shows the new firmware version.
  • Run a backup test the same week so you catch any update that broke the backup agent.
  • Write down anything you intentionally skipped — and a date you will revisit it.

What to do when a patch breaks something

It happens. A printer driver update kills printing. A firewall firmware bumps a VPN client. The answer is not to stop patching; it is to stage. Pick one or two willing users as a pilot group and let their machines update a few days ahead of everyone else. If something breaks, you find out before it spreads to the whole office.

Emergencies are different

The monthly rhythm covers normal updates. When a vendor publishes an out-of-band advisory — and 2026 has had plenty of those for firewalls, RMM tools, and common business apps — that is not a "next month" item. The rule of thumb we use:

  • Critical, internet-exposed, actively exploited: same day.
  • Critical, internet-exposed, not yet exploited: within 48 hours.
  • High severity, internal only: include in the next monthly window.

If you do not know which of your systems are internet-exposed, that is the first audit to run, before you worry about cadence.

Make it boring on purpose

The whole point is that patching should be the least interesting part of the month. A fixed window, a named owner, a short checklist, and a place to write down what you did. Done that way, it stops being a project and starts being a habit.
