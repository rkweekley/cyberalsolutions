Gaming Platform Safety: What Parents Should Set Up Before Handing Over the Controller

Saturday, May 23, 2026

Gaming has its own safety conversation, and most of it is not about the games themselves. The real questions are who can talk to your child while they play, who can add them as a friend, and what they are allowed to spend without asking. The good news is that every major platform answers those three questions in roughly the same place — the parent or family settings on the platform account.

This post covers the setup that does the heavy lifting for the rest of the year. Earlier in this series we walked through home network controls and app-level privacy settings — gaming sits next to those, with its own dashboard.

TL;DR

Gaming platforms collapse three risks into one screen — strangers via voice and chat, real money via in-game purchases, and platform-aware scams targeting young players. The fix is account setup, not surveillance. Add the child to your family group on each platform, scope chat and friends to known contacts, require approval for every purchase, and turn on two-step verification.

Why gaming is its own category

Social apps and gaming platforms look similar from a parent's seat — strangers, chat, content — but the underlying account model is different. On consoles and PC platforms, almost every safety setting is controlled at the platform-account level (Microsoft for Xbox, PSN for PlayStation, Nintendo Account, Roblox, Steam), not in each individual game.

That is a feature, not a bug. Setting up the child's account properly once protects them across every game on that platform. The catch is that most parents never see the platform settings because the child created the account on their own and the parent's account is not linked.

The other piece worth naming: Discord and other off-platform chat apps often run alongside the game. A child who has voice chat scoped down on Xbox is still talking to the same group on Discord. The privacy-settings post covers that side; this one covers the platform itself.

Account setup that does the heavy lifting

The single most useful step is putting the child's account inside a family group on each platform. Microsoft, Sony, Nintendo, and Roblox all support this; once it is in place, the parent's account is the one that approves friend requests, purchases, and certain content settings.

Two details matter here. First, the account age has to be accurate — under-13 accounts unlock a stricter default set of chat and content rules that the platform applies automatically, and lying about the age to "unlock more features" loses those protections permanently. Second, two-step verification belongs on the parent account that owns the family group; if that account is taken over, the controls on the children's accounts are taken over with it.

Voice chat and party chat

Voice chat is where most parents get surprised. A child playing a "rated E" game can still hear and talk to anyone on the same server through party chat or in-game voice. The platform settings are what gate that, not the game's age rating.

The reasonable defaults: scope voice and text chat to friends only on Xbox, PSN, and Nintendo. On Roblox, set chat to friends or off entirely for younger kids. Headphones-off is also a perfectly valid control on a shared TV — it is harder to listen to a stranger when the audio is on speakers in the living room.

Microsoft documents the Xbox controls at Xbox responsible gaming and Sony at PlayStation parental controls.

In-game purchases and virtual currency

Robux, V-Bucks, Minecoins, FIFA Points, and store credit all share one design feature: they convert real money into a number on the screen, then back into items, in a way that obscures how much is being spent. The fix is not to demonize the currency — it is to put a real-money checkpoint between the child and the store.

Concretely: turn on "ask to buy" or its equivalent on every platform so that purchases require parent approval; remove saved payment methods from the child's account; and consider a gift-card-only model for older kids who want some autonomy. Set a monthly spend cap where the platform allows one — Roblox, Microsoft, and Apple all do.

This also closes the door on a common gaming-adjacent scam: a "friend" pressuring a child to gift them Robux or skins. With approval gating in place, the answer is automatic — the parent has to approve, which gives the child a face-saving reason to say no.

Common scams worth recognizing

The scams that target young gamers reuse the same handful of patterns. Knowing them in advance makes them less effective when they show up.

The "free Robux/V-Bucks" site is the most common — a third-party page that asks for the platform login and immediately uses it to take over the account. Account-trade scams are next: someone offers a rare item in exchange for "just logging in here for a second." Phishing DMs claiming to be platform support and threatening account suspension push the child to click a link and enter credentials. Each one ends with the child's account compromised, often along with the saved payment method.

The defense is mundane and effective: two-step verification on the platform account, never logging in on a third-party site, and a household rule that platform support never DMs first. Roblox publishes the current scam patterns at the Roblox community standards page.

The six-step account setup

  • Add the child's account to your family group on each platform.
  • Set the account age accurately so platform defaults apply.
  • Scope voice, text, and party chat to friends only — or off for younger kids.
  • Require parent approval for friend requests.
  • Require parent approval for every purchase, and remove the saved card.
  • Turn on two-step verification — on the parent account first, then the child's.
