Why SMS Is the Weakest Link in MFA in 2026

Wednesday, May 13, 2026

Multi-factor authentication is the single best thing most small businesses and ministries can do to keep accounts from getting taken over. We say so often, and we mean it. But not every form of MFA is the same — and the most common one, the six-digit code that arrives by text message, is now the easiest to defeat.

If your bank, your email, your payroll, or your donation platform is still protected by a text-message code, this post is for you.

TL;DR

SMS-based MFA is better than nothing, but it is no longer enough on accounts that matter. Move banking, email, payroll, and admin accounts to a passkey or an authenticator app. Save SMS for low-risk logins where the platform offers nothing else.

Why SMS used to be fine — and isn't anymore

For years, getting a code by text was a real upgrade over a password alone. Most attackers were guessing or reusing leaked passwords, and a one-time code stopped them cold.

Three things changed:

  • SIM-swap attacks got cheap. An attacker convinces (or pays) someone at a phone carrier to move your number to their SIM. Once your number is on their phone, every text code goes to them.
  • Real-time phishing kits became standard. Modern phishing pages do not just steal your password — they relay your text-message code to the real site within seconds. You think you logged in. The attacker actually did.
  • Number-porting and account-recovery flows leak codes. A surprising amount of "MFA bypass" is just abusing carrier customer-service or platform recovery options.

What actually works in 2026, in order

If you change one thing this month, change this. Pick the strongest option each platform supports.

  • Passkeys. Built into iPhones, Androids, Macs, Windows, and most browsers. They use the device you already trust, are tied to the real website (so phishing sites cannot collect them), and there is nothing for an attacker to type. Microsoft, Google, Apple, most banks, and a growing list of business apps support them now. Free.
  • Authenticator apps. Microsoft Authenticator, Google Authenticator, Authy, or 1Password. The code lives on your phone and never travels through the cell network. Phishing kits can still trick a user into typing one in, but SIM swaps stop working entirely.
  • Hardware security keys. A small USB or NFC key (YubiKey is the common one) for high-risk accounts — your IT admin login, your domain registrar, your finance accounts. The most phishing-resistant option that exists.
  • SMS. Last resort. Use it only when a platform offers no other option, and only for low-risk accounts.

The accounts to fix first

You do not need to migrate everything in a weekend. Start where the damage would be worst.

  • Your business email — the master key for almost every other account's password reset.
  • Banking, payroll, and accounting (QuickBooks, Gusto, your bank's business portal).
  • Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace global admin accounts.
  • Donation, giving, or e-commerce platforms — where attackers can redirect funds.
  • Your domain registrar and DNS host. If an attacker controls your domain, they control your email and your website.

What about the codes you already get?

Two practical guardrails while you migrate:

  • Add a port-out PIN with your cell carrier. Verizon, AT&T, and T-Mobile all offer one. It blocks casual SIM-swap attempts.
  • Treat any unexpected MFA code as a red flag. If you did not just try to log in, do not type the code anywhere — go change that password immediately.

What to expect when you switch

Setting up a passkey or authenticator app takes about five minutes per account. Most platforms now walk you through it. The friction shows up once: when you replace a phone, you re-enroll. Backup codes — printed and kept somewhere safe — make that painless.

If your team is going to balk at any of this, frame it as fewer interruptions, not more. A passkey is a tap-to-log-in. An authenticator app is faster than waiting for a text. The change is nearly always less work, not more.

One more thing for ministries and small offices

Shared accounts — the "office@" email everyone uses, the donation-platform login that three volunteers know — are where SMS MFA does the most damage. The phone number on those accounts is usually somebody's personal cell, often someone who left two years ago. Audit those first. If you cannot remove the shared login entirely, at least move the second factor onto an authenticator app on a phone the current administrator owns.

The honest version

SMS MFA is not "wrong." It is just no longer the bar. The accounts that would actually hurt you to lose deserve a stronger second factor, and in 2026 the stronger options are free, fast, and built into the devices you already use.
