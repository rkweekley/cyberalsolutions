Sextortion and Financial Scams Targeting Teens: What Parents Should Know

Wednesday, May 27, 2026

This is the post in the series we wish parents did not need. Earlier we covered warning signs, network controls, app privacy settings, and gaming platform safety. Those layers exist because of what this post covers: a category of online scam aimed squarely at teenagers, designed to move very fast and to make them feel like there is no way out.

The intent here is not alarm. It is preparation. A parent who has heard the playbook once is in a much better position to be the calm voice in the room if it ever comes up.

TL;DR

A growing pattern targets teens through direct messages on social and gaming platforms. The scammer builds rapid trust, gets a private image or a payment, then uses it to extort more. The right response is the same every time: stop responding, do not pay, do not delete, save evidence, and report it. The FBI's IC3, the NCMEC CyberTipline, and NCMEC's Take It Down service are the destinations. None of them will judge a teen for what happened.

How the playbook works

Most cases follow a recognizable arc. A new account — often posing as a peer of a similar age — starts a conversation through a DM, a friend request, or a comment on a recent post. The first messages are warm and unremarkable. Trust builds over hours or sometimes minutes, not weeks.

The escalation comes in one of two directions. In the image-based version, the scammer either sends a fake image first and asks for one in return, or pressures the teen into sharing one. The moment they have it, the tone changes immediately and the demand for money begins, usually in gift cards, payment apps, or cryptocurrency. In the financial version — the same playbook, different ending — the scammer asks the teen to "help receive a package" or "move some money" and uses the teen as a money mule, leaving them holding the legal consequences.

The FBI has been publishing public advisories on this pattern for several years; the current guidance lives at FBI: Sextortion. The shape of the attack is consistent enough that the platforms themselves have started flagging it in onboarding flows.

Warning signs parents can spot

The technical signs are usually quieter than the emotional ones. A teen who is being extorted often becomes intensely protective of the phone, cycles through panic and withdrawal, makes unexplained requests for money or gift cards, or shows new late-night activity from accounts you have not seen before.

One subtle sign is worth naming directly: a sudden, out-of-character request to buy gift cards "for a friend." Gift cards are the dominant payment method in these scams because they are non-reversible and hard to trace. Any urgent gift-card request from a teen, especially with secrecy attached, is worth a calm follow-up question.

The conversation to have before anything happens

The hardest part of preparing a teen for this is making sure they will tell you. The single most useful thing a parent can say, on a normal day with no incident attached, is some version of: "If anyone online ever pressures you for an image or for money, you can come straight to me, no phone taken away, no anger. I will help you handle it. The people doing this count on you not telling anyone — telling someone is how it ends."

The reason that conversation matters is the speed of the scam. Teens are pressured to decide in minutes. A teen who has rehearsed where to go in advance is much less likely to comply, and much more likely to come to a parent before the situation escalates.

It is also worth saying explicitly that this is not their fault. The scammers are professionals running this at scale; the teen is not failing some test of judgment they should have passed.

What to do in the first hour if it happens

The first hour is where good outcomes are made. The protocol is simple, and parents can follow it without specialist help.

First, stop responding. Engagement of any kind — including arguing, pleading, or threatening — feeds the scammer's leverage. Second, do not pay. Paying does not stop it; in almost every documented case the demands escalate after the first payment. Third, do not delete the conversation. The messages, usernames, and URLs are the evidence law enforcement and the platforms need.

Then save evidence: take screenshots of the conversation, the profile, and any payment requests, including the timestamps. Block the account on the platform after the screenshots, not before, and use the platform's report tool to flag it.

Where to report

There are three destinations that matter, and they are not exclusive — report to all of them.

The FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) is the federal reporting channel for sextortion and online financial scams. The NCMEC CyberTipline is the national hotline for crimes against children online; reports there feed into the same investigative pipelines and are routed to the right jurisdiction. NCMEC also operates Take It Down, a free service that helps remove sexually explicit images of minors from participating platforms — it works from a hash of the image, so the original never has to leave the device.

Local law enforcement is also a reasonable call, particularly if the teen is in immediate distress. The federal channels and the local channel can both run at the same time.

The five-step "if it happens" protocol

  • Stop responding — do not argue, plead, or threaten.
  • Do not pay — payment escalates, it does not end it.
  • Save evidence — screenshots, usernames, URLs, timestamps. Do not delete the conversation.
  • Report to the FBI IC3, the NCMEC CyberTipline, and the platform itself. Use Take It Down for image removal.
  • Take care of the teen — mental-health support matters as much as the technical response. Local resources count.

Where this fits in the bigger picture

The first four posts in this series exist so that the playbook described here has fewer entry points: a private account, friend-only DMs, supervised gaming chat, and a household where the warning signs are recognizable. None of those layers is perfect. The point of having them stacked together is so that, if it ever does happen, the response is fast and unambiguous — and the teen is not facing it alone.
